Community Assembly and Species Coexistence

For over a century, field ecologists have been characterizing patterns in ecological communities and trying to draw theoretical inferences from the resulting data.

Central Questions:

  • Why do species occur in specific locations?

  • Why do some species coexist while others do not?

  • Environmental filtering:
    • Ecologically similar species should coexist in ecologically similar environments.
  • Limiting similarity:
    • Ecologically dissimilar species should coexist because too similar species competing for the same resources cannot stably coexist.
  • Neutral theory:
    • Dispersal and stochastic demographic processes explain species coexistence and species differences are not important.

Russo et al. 2005, Journal of Ecology

Which community is more diverse?

How to measure species characteristics?

Photosynthetic rates

Directly measuring the physiological traits of every species in a forest containing hundreds of coexisting species takes substantial effort. This motivated the idea of using phylogenetic relatedness as a proxy for ecological similarity.

Faith’s Index (PD)

Faith 1992, Biological Conservation

  • Total branch length = 18
  • PD is the sum of the lengths of all those branches that are members of the corresponding minimum spanning path
  • PD is the phylogenetic analogue of taxon richness and is expressed as the number of tree units which are found in a sample
  • PD will correlate with species richness

PD, MPD and MNTD: what do we measure?

  • PD: sum the connecting branches, counting each branch once.
  • MPD (Mean Pairwise Distance): average the path lengths over all species pairs.
  • MNTD (Mean Nearest Taxon Distance): average the distance to each species’ nearest neighbor.

Example: A–B = 1 + 1 = 2; A–C = 1 + 1 + 2 = 4.

MPD includes both pairs. For A’s contribution to MNTD, only its nearest neighbor B counts.

Faith 1992, Biological Conservation · Webb 2000, The American Naturalist

PD is a total that counts each shared branch only once. MPD and MNTD are averages of path lengths between species; neither is PD divided by species richness. Trace the branch lengths on the left to check A–B and A–C. The MNTD example shows only A’s contribution. For the community value, find the nearest-neighbor distance for every species and then take the average.

Same richness, different aspects of community structure

Community PD MPD MNTD
A, B, C, D 15 7.67 5.00
A, B, D, E 14 8.67 2.00
  • Higher PD in A, B, C, D: more evolutionary history in total, including the isolated lineage D.
  • Higher MPD in A, B, D, E: more pairs span the deep split between the A–B and D–E lineages.
  • Lower MNTD in A, B, D, E: every species has a close relative, forming two pairs within the community.

A community can contain distant lineages and close relatives at the same time.

Faith 1992, Biological Conservation · Webb 2000, The American Naturalist · Tucker et al. 2017, Biological Reviews

Both communities contain four species, but species identity changes the total evolutionary history, overall separation, and arrangement of close relatives. In ABCD, D has no close relative, so MNTD is higher. In ABDE, every species has a relative at distance 2, so MNTD is lower. Meanwhile, the number of pairs spanning the deep split increases from three to four, raising MPD. PD decreases from 15 to 14. Ecologically, a community can span the phylogeny while every species also has a close relative within the community. Which community is “more diverse” therefore depends on whether the question emphasizes total evolutionary history for conservation, overall phylogenetic separation, or nearest-neighbor relationships. These values describe phylogenetic structure; they do not identify environmental filtering or competition on their own. Assess clustering or overdispersion against a null model that accounts for the species pool, richness, and other relevant constraints. Interpreting phylogenetic distance as niche difference also requires correspondence with traits, leading into the Anole example.

Blomberg et al. 2003, Evolution

The original two-panel figure is preserved without changing its content or data. It compares two temperature traits in 12 species of Australian skinks. The figure reports K = 0.453 and K = 0.101; the explanation rounds these to 0.45 and 0.10.

Blomberg’s K = 1 is the expectation under Brownian motion. K < 1 indicates less phylogenetic signal than expected under Brownian motion. The P values come from randomization tests of phylogenetic signal, not tests of agreement with Brownian motion. Thus K < 1 and a significant randomization test can occur together, as for preferred body temperature.

For optimal temperature for sprinting, P = 0.167 means that significant phylogenetic signal was not detected; it does not establish that phylogenetic signal is absent. Check the trait relevant to the ecological question before using relatedness as its proxy.

The phylogenetic middleman problem

Swenson 2013, Ecography

  • Phylogeny as a proxy for the functional or ecological similarity of species.

  • Measure traits → show phylogenetic signal → use phylogeny to infer trait-based processes.

  • Compared to simply measuring the trait dispersion, this approach is very indirect.

  • If the traits are measured, quantify trait dispersion directly.

This does not reject phylogenetic diversity itself. PD describes the amount of evolutionary history, whereas trait distances describe differences in the measured traits. If trait dispersion is the question and the relevant traits can be measured, quantify it directly rather than using phylogeny as an intermediary.

Petchey & Gaston’s functional diversity (FD)

  • Functional diversity: differences in species’ functional traits.
  • Functional dendrogram: built from measured trait differences (a phylogeny represents shared ancestry).
  • Petchey & Gaston’s FD: total branch length connecting the community’s species.
  • Parallels Faith’s PD, but uses trait differences instead of evolutionary history.

Petchey & Gaston 2002, Ecology Letters

Functional diversity is an umbrella concept; FD, introduced here, is one particular metric. Calculate distances between species from trait data, then cluster them to produce a dendrogram. Branch lengths do not represent evolutionary time. The following convex hull example measures functional richness using the extent of occupied trait space rather than a dendrogram.

Community assembly and trait distribution

Cornwell & Ackerly 2009, Ecological Monographs

A narrower range of trait values and more even spacing of species within that range are different properties. The former is a prediction of environmental filtering, whereas the latter is a prediction of limiting similarity. The next empirical example examines both.

Different processes can produce the same pattern. For example, clustering can arise from environmental filtering or from competitive hierarchy, where only species with competitively advantageous traits remain. Patterns do not uniquely identify processes. In addition to null-model comparisons, we need hypotheses that connect traits to processes.

Competitive hierarchy

[1] \(t_A\) and \(t_B\) are the functional trait values of species A and B

Kunstler et al. 2012, Ecology Letters

Limiting similarity

Competitive interaction strengths between species will increase with decreasing niche distance, measured as their absolute traits distance \(|t_A - t_B|\)

Competitive hierarchy

Competitive effects of species A on species B will increase with increasing \(t_A - t_B\).

Tree height illustrates a trait for which a larger value can confer a competitive advantage. On the left, greater similarity means stronger competition, so the absolute difference matters. On the right, which species has the larger value matters, so the signed difference matters. This is not a general rule that larger values are advantageous for every trait.

Community traits: mean vs. diversity

  • Community-weighted mean (CWM)

    \(\mathrm{CWM} = \Sigma p_i t_i\)

    Which strategy dominates?

  • Functional diversity

    How different are the coexisting strategies?

The mean and the spread can change independently.

pi: relative abundance of species i · ti: trait value of species i

The weights p_i may represent relative abundance, biomass, or cover. CWM asks where the community’s “center” lies; functional diversity asks how different the species are from one another. Community 2 has the same mean but greater spread, whereas community 3 has the same spread but a shifted mean. The earlier FD and convex hull examples measured differences among species. We now turn to the community mean: a change in species abundances can shift CWM even if species trait values stay fixed. The next BCI example asks what causes such a shift.

Community averages can mislead without species‑level context

Katabuchi et al. 2017, Ecology · Swenson et al. 2020, Ecological Monographs

  • Study site: 50-ha forest dynamics plot, Barro Colorado Island, Panama.
  • Community-level pattern: Increasing CWM wood density appears consistent with a response to drier conditions.
  • Species-level explanation: Just two species account for ~60% of the temporal CWM shift, suggesting that species-specific processes may drive the apparent community trend.

Two of approximately 300 species (~0.7%) account for ~60% of the temporal CWM shift. Species-specific pathogens were suggested as a possible explanation, rather than a community-wide climate response. The next example shows the same problem for diversity indices: an increase can hide which species are gained or lost.

Does greater functional richness mean better habitat quality?

Kambach et al. 2026, Nature Communications

This pairs with the preceding BCI example: there, a shift in community mean was driven by two species; here, diversity gains are driven by generalist and non-native species. In both cases, an aggregate index hides which species are gained or lost. It also revisits functional richness (convex hull): because FRic increases with species richness, ask whether the FRic gain reflects more than the richness gain. The main-text Methods compute FRic from 14 traits scaled to unit variance and do not describe a richness-matched null model. CWM niche width (+0.004) applies the CWM introduced on the previous slides. The cover of Red List species also increased (+0.39), so do not present the figure as uniform degradation; the point is that index increases need species-level context. The dataset contains 199,282 observations in 57,390 vegetation-plot time series. Fourteen traits were used to calculate functional richness, evenness and divergence. Mean annual relative changes include species richness +0.22%, functional richness +0.31%, functional divergence +0.14%, MPD +0.59% and MNTD +0.70%; functional evenness had no significant overall trend. These are changes relative to each metric’s baseline, not a common measure of ecological importance. Read each estimate with its uncertainty; do not rank metrics by their percentage increase. The wetland explanation is an interpretation offered by the authors, not an experimentally demonstrated cause. Drying and disturbance may allow shrubs, trees and generalist grasses to establish while changing the original habitat. Habitat-specific European gamma diversity showed no general trend; local gains should not be equated with regional gains. The study does not directly establish declining beta diversity. The records span a century, but individual plots were not all monitored for a century: 55% of time series have only two observations, and 5–10 years is the most common interval. Resurvey selection may underrepresent completely destroyed habitats. Trait values include gap-filled species data and do not capture all within-species variation. For discussion: if a drying wetland gains widespread grass and shrub species, its trait space may expand without recovery of wetland specialists. Ask students to distinguish the measured index from the conservation interpretation. Generalists were the “winners” here. Can traits predict which species win under environmental change? Can traits predict short-term drought response?

Fig. 2, all-species panel. Cover change accounts for changes in control plots; higher values indicate greater drought resistance.

  • Experiment: ~1 year of extreme drought at 63 sites; 421 herbaceous species, 661 populations.
  • LES expectation: Conservative leaves (low leaf N, low SLA) → greater drought resistance?
  • Result: The opposite: higher leaf N was associated with greater resistance (drought escape?).
  • But the all-species model explained only 6% of variation.

Traits covary strongly with each other, yet predict performance weakly.

Worthy et al. 2026, Nature Ecology & Evolution

Source: Worthy_et_al_2026.pdf, Fig. 2 (PDF p. 3), Results and Methods; https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-026-02989-4. The figure shows only the original all-species panel. The other growth-form and lifespan panels are not shown. Connect to LES: before revealing the result, ask students whether low leaf N and low SLA, associated with resource conservation, should predict resistance. Then reveal the direction (opposite to the expectation), and finally the R². This study tests that prediction against experimentally imposed drought in grasslands and shrublands. It does not measure tree responses or long-term drought survival. The response is a before–after, control–impact change in percentage cover: (drought after − drought before) − (control after − control before). Higher values indicate less negative or more positive cover change under drought relative to the control. Cover integrates multiple processes and is not a direct measurement of physiological tolerance or survival. Nine traits were tested using Bayesian mixed models with species and site effects. In the pooled analysis, leaf N was positively associated with drought resistance. The reported R² = 6% belongs to the full all-species model, not to leaf N alone, and is not an out-of-sample validation score. The scatter illustrates why a supported average relationship need not predict individual population responses accurately. Forbs also showed positive associations with height and root tissue density; no trait associations were significant in graminoids. Trait–trait and trait–environment interactions mattered. Do not infer that differences in statistical significance alone demonstrate different effect sizes between groups. The authors discuss drought escape as one possible explanation for acquisitive traits performing well during short droughts. Species-level trait data included imputed values and did not represent local plasticity at every site. Trait effects also depended on growth form, lifespan and environmental conditions. These results motivate specifying the response, timescale, plant group and environmental context when selecting traits. Transition: why might traits predict so weakly? One reason: a species-mean, bulk trait can combine components with different physiological meanings, and ignores variation within species. The next slide examines this for LMA.

Why leaf trait relationships differ within and among species

  • Across species: Leaf lifespan increases strongly with LMA, whereas area-based photosynthesis shows a weak relationship.
  • Within species: Area-based photosynthesis increases with LMA in the studied Panama assemblages.
  • Proposed mechanism: LMA variation reflects different contributions of photosynthetic and structural tissues.

Osnas, Katabuchi et al. 2018, PNAS

Source: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1803989115 Following Worthy et al.: species-mean traits assume that one relationship holds both among and within species. Here, the LMA–photosynthesis relationship differs between the two. The global and Panama datasets also differ in geographical scale, so do not attribute every contrast solely to within-species versus among-species variation. Return to the 2013 normalization example: the same change in LMA can have different physiological meanings depending on its components. The proposed hypothesis is that variation in photosynthetically active mass contributes strongly to LMA variation between sun and shade leaves within species, whereas variation in structural mass contributes strongly across species. The data are consistent with these predictions; the claim is not that identical relationships hold in every within-species and among-species comparison. Structural mass relates to strength and leaf lifespan. Rather than applying correlations among species means directly to responses within species, consider the components that generate LMA. The next example extends this reasoning to physiological prediction.

Separating LMA components improves physiological prediction

  • Bulk LMA combines metabolic and structural components.
  • Metabolic mass (LMAₘ) is more strongly associated with photosynthesis and respiration per leaf area.
  • Structural mass (LMAₛ) is strongly associated with leaf lifespan.

Separating metabolic and structural components reveals distinct functional relationships obscured by bulk LMA.

Katabuchi et al. 2025, Oecologia

Reading order: for photosynthesis, compare (a) LMA with (b) LMAm; for leaf lifespan, compare (g) LMA with (i) LMAs. LMAm is photosynthetically active (metabolic) mass; LMAs is structural mass. Move from total LMA to its component parts. Present this as an example of improved physiological prediction. The logic is process-informed: the trait is defined from the processes that generate it (metabolic vs structural mass) rather than taken as a bulk measurement. The prediction target here is physiology; the study does not demonstrate prediction of vital rates, coexistence or invasion growth rates. It also does not resolve the 6% in Worthy et al., whose target was cover change under drought. The next slide places this example on the axis from traits to coexistence.

From physiological prediction to species coexistence

Conceptual illustration

  • Traits alone are insufficient to predict species coexistence.
  • Predictive models must link traits and physiology to demographic rates and species interactions.
  • Invasion growth rates (growth when rare) provide a direct link to coexistence theory.
  • LMAₘ / LMAₛ: process-informed in logic, but still a trait → left end of this axis.

Levine et al. 2024, Trends in Ecology & Evolution

Invasion growth rate is the population growth rate of a species introduced at low abundance into an established community. A positive value means it can increase when rare, a condition relevant to coexistence. This is a conceptual illustration; it does not imply that predictive performance actually reaches 100%. In this figure, process-informed metrics sit between vital rates and niche/fitness differences, closer to coexistence than traits. Ask students where the previous examples sit: LMAm/LMAs separates a trait by process but remains at the “functional traits” position with physiology as its target; Worthy et al. linked traits to drought performance and explained 6%. Improving physiological prediction does not by itself predict species coexistence. Consider the connections to individual growth, survival, reproduction, and population growth under competition. Return to Worthy et al.: what is missing from the remaining 94%? Demography, species interactions and environmental context. When choosing metrics that reflect mechanisms, specify the prediction target. Physiology, population dynamics, and coexistence are different prediction targets.

Summary 🍺

  • Why do we use trait and phylogenetic diversity?

    • To quantify ecological similarities and biodiversity dimensions.

    • Be careful when mapping many dimensions of biodiversity to a few indices.

    • Patterns alone do not identify processes; compare them with null models.

  • Trait-based approaches

    • Moving ecology towards more quantitative and predictive methods.

    • Describing diversity is not the same as predicting dynamics or coexistence.

    • Better metrics may be needed, beyond simply measuring traits.

Can normalization create correlations with LMA?

1. Mass normalization

\[ A_{\mathrm{mass}}=\frac{A_{\mathrm{area}}}{LMA} \]

\[ \log A_{\mathrm{mass}}=\log A_{\mathrm{area}}-\log LMA \]

2. Covariance with LMA

General identity:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{Cov}(\log A_{\mathrm{mass}},\log LMA)\\ &=\mathrm{Cov}(\log A_{\mathrm{area}},\log LMA)\\ &\quad-\mathrm{Var}(\log LMA) \end{aligned} \]

If log area-based photosynthesis is uncorrelated with log LMA:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{Cov}(\log A_{\mathrm{mass}},\log LMA)\\ &=-\mathrm{Var}(\log LMA) \end{aligned} } \]

  • Area-based photosynthesis is converted to mass-based photosynthesis by dividing by LMA.
  • This introduces a shared mathematical dependence on LMA.
  • Even if area-based photosynthesis is uncorrelated with LMA (on log scale), mass-based photosynthesis will be negatively correlated with LMA.

Key takeaway:

A negative relationship between mass-based photosynthesis and LMA does not necessarily indicate a biological trade-off.

The general identity holds without this assumption.

Osnas et al. 2013, Science
